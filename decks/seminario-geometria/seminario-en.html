before we start · how to read the symbols

The conventions that hold for the whole journey

Mathematics writes with conventions that are almost never said out loud. Said out loud, they stop being a barrier: you start decoding a new symbol without waiting for anyone to introduce it.

\(A\), \(B\), \(P\)slanted capital — a point in space
\(E\), \(M\), \(R\)a capital also names the big object: the whole space, a table of numbers, a relation. Which one it is, the sheet says on the spot
\(\vec v\), \(\overrightarrow{AB}\)arrow on top — a vector: the family of arrows, not a single arrow
\(x\), \(y\), \(z\)slanted lowercase — the coordinates: width, depth, height
\(k\), \(t\), \(h\)lowercase is also a plain number: the one that stretches, the instant, the height
\(\alpha\), \(\theta\), \(\rho\)Greek letter — what is being measured: an angle, an angle, a distance. Never the subject of the sheet; always its measure. Read as "alpha", "theta", "rho"
\(\mathbb{R}\)hollow letter — all the real numbers. Read as "R"
\(\|\vec v\|\) and \(|k|\)double bars mean length — the norm; single bars mean the number without its sign. Alike on the page, different in what they measure
sin, cos, tanupright letters — the name of an operation, not a letter standing for a number

the shape of the letter already tells you which family the symbol belongs to. Once the family is recognised, a symbol you have never seen stops being an obstacle: you read its shape and already know what to expect.

Station 1 · matrices

What an image is: a table of numbers

Each little square is a pixel, and each pixel holds one number: 0 is black, 255 is white, and everything in between is grey.

In mathematics this table has a name: an \(m \times n\) matrix — \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns of numbers.

“em by en.”

Each entry is called \(a_{ij}\): the number that lives in row \(i\), column \(j\). The little house beside it is an \(8 \times 12\) matrix — and the highlighted cell is \(a_{3,5} = 70\): third row, fifth column.

“a i j.”

Station 1 · matrices

The order of the matrix is what everyday language calls resolution

Changing resolution means changing the order of the matrix — and the “pixel count” that gets advertised is the area of that table: rows times columns.

the sheet you are looking at holds 1 011 712 numbers per channel. A 4K frame holds eight times that. Same idea, different scales.

Mind the order: the matrix says rows × columns — height first. That is why Full HD is the \(1080 \times 1920\) matrix, even though the trade advertises “1920×1080”, width first. Same table, two grammars.

Station 1 · matrices

The colour we see: multiply and add

+ + =

Each grey channel is multiplied by its primary colour and the three are added — light adds. This is the arithmetic, done pixel by pixel:

\(I = R\cdot(1,0,0) + G\cdot(0,1,0) + B\cdot(0,0,1)\)

In a single pixel: the lower-left corner of the window in the three previous tables holds R = 237, G = 157 and B = 74 — plenty of red, medium green, little blue. The arithmetic returns this orange: it is the glow of twilight just below the horizon.

“read as: the image I is channel R times pure red, plus channel G times pure green, plus channel B times pure blue” — the sum above reconstructs the original image exactly, pixel by pixel (checked by program). Three tables of numbers, three colours, one sum: this is the whole final image.

Station 1 → Station 2 · the bridge

The tables are flat — the world they show is deep

Computer graphics — in film, in games, in the craft of page 2 — runs the opposite way from what we have just seen: first it builds a world in three dimensions (objects, camera, lights) and only at the end does it flatten that world into tables of pixels. That flattening has a name: render.

To build that world, every point needs an address in space — three numbers, not two. And the tool that builds those addresses starts out humble: an arrow.

No figure in this seminar floats in a void. They all live on the same stage, and that stage is declared before anything is drawn — the way opening a new file in a 3D program shows you, first of all, the ground grid.

Oriented segment: an arrow with a name

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

1. Oriented segment in a space \(E\).
Let \(A, B \in E\) be points. The segment \((A,B)\) is oriented when we take \(A\) as the origin and \(B\) as the endpoint.

it is a piece of a line with an arrowhead. It starts at \(A\) and ends at \(B\). Order matters: \((A,B)\) and \((B,A)\) are different arrows — they point opposite ways.

\(E\)space: the set of ALL points (our 3D)
\(A \in E\)read as “\(A\) belongs to \(E\)” — \(A\) is a point of space
\((A,B)\)read as “segment A-B” — the arrow that goes from \(A\) to \(B\)

Station 2 · vectors

Direction: the line the arrow follows

An arrow has three attributes, and this is the first. Without it there is no way to say when two arrows are “the same” — which is the next step.

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

Direction of \((A,B)\) is the angle \(\alpha\) the segment makes with the \(xy\) plane. Arrows with the same angle run along the same track: they have the same direction — which says nothing about sense, and nothing about norm.

“alpha.”

The two statements do not contradict each other: the angle identifies the track, and the track is the direction. Giving the angle is a way of pointing at which of the infinitely many tracks you mean — like giving the postcode of a street without the postcode being the street.

direction is NOT a number and NOT an angle: it is “which track the arrow runs on”. Two arrows on parallel tracks have the same direction, even when they are far apart and point opposite ways.

There are three attributes — direction, sense and length. This slide settles only the first: the angle \(\alpha\) with the \(xy\) plane, or, equivalently, the supporting line and every line parallel to it. The other two come next.

Station 2 · vectors

When two arrows are “the same”: equipollence

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

2. Equipollent oriented segments. \((A,B)\) and \((C,D)\) are equipollent when:
(i) \((A,B) \parallel (C,D)\) — they have the same direction;
(ii) they have the same sense;
(iii) they have the same norm:

\[\|(A,B)\| = \sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2 + (y_1-y_2)^2 + (z_1-z_2)^2}\]

“the norm of segment A-B equals the square root of: x-one minus x-two, squared; plus y-one minus y-two, squared; plus z-one minus z-two, squared.” The symbol \(\parallel\) reads as “is parallel to.”

the frightening formula is just the Pythagoras you already know from school, used twice — the ruler that measures any arrow in space. Keep it: it comes back at the end under another name, \(\rho\) (“rho”).

from the record to the pixel
Station 2 · vectors

How to find out in practice: one test for each attribute

The definition says what it is. These three say how to check it, by hand, with no arithmetic at all:

  • direction — extend both lines: if they never cross (parallel), same direction;
  • sense — the X test: join origin to origin (A–C) and endpoint to endpoint (B–D); if the lines do not cross, same sense; if they form an X, opposite senses;
  • norm — measure from \(A\) to \(B\): it is the length of the segment, a number that is never negative, in the unit of the scene (here, metres).

“size” is loose speech; the exact word is length. And the three tests hold together: failing one is already enough for the arrows not to be the same.

Station 2 · vectors

The three rules of “being equivalent”

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

This defines an equivalence relation: \(R\) is an equivalence relation when:
\(\forall a\): \(aRa\) — reflexive;
\(\forall a,b\): \(aRb \Rightarrow bRa\) — symmetric;
\(\forall a,b,c\): \(aRb,\ bRc \Rightarrow aRc\) — transitive.

“for every a: a relates to a” · “for all a and b: if a relates to b, then b relates to a” · “for all a, b and c: if a relates to b, and b to c, then a relates to c.”

\(\forall\)“for every” — holds without exception
\(aRb\)“\(a\) relates to \(b\)” (here: is equipollent to)
\(\Rightarrow\)“implies” — if the left-hand side holds, the right-hand side holds

“equivalent” = equal for what concerns us here. The three rules only guarantee that this “equal” behaves like a real equality: everything is equal to itself; if \(a\) is equal to \(b\), then \(b\) is equal to \(a\); and the shortcut holds. Equipollence passes all three — it may carry the title.

Station 2 · vectors

The naming: a vector is the name of the family of arrows

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10 Equivalence class: \[\overrightarrow{(A,B)} = \{(x,y) ;\ (x,y)\, R \,(A,B)\}\]

Vector \(\vec{v}\) is an equivalence class of equipollent oriented segments.

“the class of segment A-B is the set of pairs x-y such that x-y relates to A-B” — the braces { } read as “the set of”, and the semicolon as “such that”. \(\vec{v}\) reads as “vector v” (the little arrow on top marks it: this is a vector, not a number).

the “class” is the bag holding ALL arrows equipollent to one another — the formula reads as “the set of \((x,y)\) that relate to \((A,B)\)”. We give the bag ONE name: \(\vec{v}\). A vector is not an arrow — it is the whole family. That is why it can “live” anywhere in space.

Adding is splicing: the end of one is the start of the next

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

Sum of vectors:

\[(A,B) + (B,D) = (A,D)\]

“segment A-B plus segment B-D equals segment A-D.”

the end of the first arrow is the start of the second, and the result is the direct shortcut — the third side of the triangle.

Notice that the middle path disappears: whoever looks only at the ends cannot tell which way was walked.

Station 2 · vectors

Multiplying by a number: stretching and shrinking

from the blackboard · Prof. Rosivaldo · Aug 10

Scalar times vector: \(k \in \mathbb{R}\), \(k\vec{v} \parallel \vec{v}\), with \(\|k\vec{v}\| = |k|\cdot\|\vec{v}\|\):
\(|k| < 1\) “shrinks” · \(|k| > 1\) “stretches”.

“k times vector v is parallel to vector v, and the norm of k v equals the absolute value of k times the norm of v” (absolute value = the number without its sign).

multiplying by a number changes only the length — the track is the same. A negative number flips the arrow around; the track stays the same.

These two operations are the whole foundation: the rest of linear algebra follows from them.

Station 2 · vectors

Length does not add: the triangle inequality

splicing two arrows gives a shortcut — and a shortcut is always shorter than going around. Only when both point the same way is there no shortcut to gain, and then the lengths do add.

With \(u = (3,1,0)\) and \(v = (1,3,2)\): \(\|u\| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\) and \(\|v\| = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74\) — but \(\|u+v\| = 6\), not \(6.90\).

“the norm of u equals the square root of ten, approximately three point one six”; “the norm of u plus v equals six.”

\[\|u+v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|\]

“the norm of u plus v is less than or equal to the norm of u plus the norm of v.”

The coordinates add; the length, does not.

Choosing an origin: every point becomes three numbers

So far, no numbers — only drawing. Analytic geometry begins the moment we choose an origin and three axes: from then on every point \(P\) gets an address \((x, y, z)\).

“point P gets an address x, y, zee” — the commas separate the three coordinates, in the order width, depth, height.

it is like giving the points a postcode. The drawing does not change — it is we who become able to calculate with it.

The author's choice, and it will matter all the way to the last slide: the origin is the observer — the eye of whoever sees the scene, 1.6 m above the ground.

The object of study: a cube resting on the ground, in front of the observer. One colour per vertex (corner).

Station 3 · coordinates

The horizon is a plane — and it is what gives the axes their letters

With the origin at the eye, two horizontal planes organise the scene: the eye plane (\(z = 0\)), which passes through the observer parallel to the ground, and the ground (\(z = -h\)), with \(h = 1.6\) m — the height of the eye.

“zee equals zero” and “zee equals minus aitch.”

the horizon is not a line that exists in the world: it is the plane at the height of your own eyes, seen edge-on — which is why it rises and falls with you, and why it disappears when you lie down.

Mind the change of letter: in the plane, the vertical axis is \(y\) — that is what school teaches. On gaining the third dimension, the whole plane becomes the ground and the one that rises becomes \(z\). Nothing broke: the number of axes changed.

Station 3 · coordinates

The angle returns: why the horizon never arrives

A point on the ground seen \(\alpha\) below the horizon lies at

\[\rho = \frac{h}{\operatorname{sen}\alpha}, \qquad s = \frac{h}{\operatorname{tg}\alpha}\]

“rho equals aitch over sine of alpha; ess equals aitch over tangent of alpha.”

\(\alpha\)the angle between the ray reaching the point and the plane of the horizon
\(\rho\)the distance from the eye to the point — in a straight line
\(s\)the distance on the ground, from the observer's feet to the point

the closer to the horizon you look, the smaller \(\alpha\) becomes and the farther away is the ground you are seeing. At the horizon, \(\alpha = 0\): the sine vanishes, and the distance goes to infinity.

This closes the conversation opened at station 1: this \(\alpha\) is the elevation, one of the two measures a direction requires in space. The other is which way along the horizon it points. Two measures, never one.

Station 3 · coordinates

Moving the whole shape = a single computation

The \(k\vec v\) from the blackboard, applied to all 8 columns at once: \(k \cdot M\) — the whole cube shrinks or stretches together.

And rotating? Rotation is a table too — the rotation matrix — multiplying ours:

\[ R\,M, \qquad R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\[2pt] 0 & 1 & 0 \\[2pt] -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]

“R times M; R is the three-by-three matrix: first row, cosine of theta, zero, sine of theta; second row, zero, one, zero; third row, minus sine of theta, zero, cosine of theta.” \(\theta\) (“theta”) is the angle of the turn; sine and cosine are the ones from school.

multiplying matrices = applying the SAME recipe to every column at once. Follow the colours in the figure: each vertex landed exactly where the arithmetic sent it. It is this arithmetic, billions of times per second, that a graphics card performs.

Station 4 · equations — the arithmetic inside the eye

Inside the eye: one ray per pixel, one computation per ray

The light of the world converges to a point inside the eye — from every direction one ray arrives. Seeing is asking, direction by direction: “what is the first thing along here?” The pixel is that question.

The answer is a linear system: the ray leaves the eye, \(P = t\cdot d\), and the surface lives in a plane (the face of the cube: \(y = 3.5\)). Where does the ray pierce it? \(t\,d_y = 3.5\) (“read as: tee times dee-y equals three point five”). At school it was two equations and two unknowns; here there are three — the same idea.

And depth hides: along the same ray, only the first surface answers — whatever lies behind it never reaches the eye.

And this stage does not stay in the figure. It is this station that exports it as a depth map and hands it to the generative model: the ground grid becomes, over there, the number that tells each pixel how far away it is.

Whatever lies at the same distance \(\rho\) from the eye lives on the same shell: on the ground, concentric circles of radius \(\sqrt{\rho^2 - h^2}\) (“read as: the square root of rho squared minus aitch squared” — Pythagoras, again). It is the ruler that serves every gaze.

Station 5 · pixels

A photograph is a cut in time

The shutter freezes a single instant: of the whole moving world one slice remains — of time and of space.

In that cut the 3D world goes half invisible, half confused: there is what fell outside the frame, what the edge cut through the middle, and what was left hidden behind whatever came first.

And the cut has a natural ruler: slicing the scene into planes parallel to the picture plane — the depth \(d\) of each thing is the slice it lives in. The square that faces the camera lives entirely inside a single slice — which is why the plane-based depth map “looks like” the camera's own view of it; the rotated squares cross slices.

Recovering the world from the photograph is hard even for us. That is exactly what we will ask of the rulers ahead: to say, from the cut alone, how far away each thing was.

Station 5 · pixels

What the ruler does not carry: the map is 2.5D

A depth map stores one value per pixel: the distance to the first surface the ray meets.

\( d(x,y) \) — one scalar per ray

“dee of x and y.”

where the surface passes behind itself, the map records a step — but the surface behind does not get in. What is missing is continuity across the crossing, and that is what separates a Möbius strip — the band with a half-twist, which has one side only — from an ordinary ring, which has two.

Measured, not assumed. Pre-registered round of 2026-08-12, 32 sheets, blind judging: the map delivered silhouette, position and depth, and none of the 8 sheets held the half-twist.

The ruler itself declared the round ambiguous — the predictions stamped in advance were not confirmed. What survives is not a victory, it is a distinction: a ruler that measures distance does not carry topology — the part of mathematics that studies what survives being bent without being torn: how many holes, how many sides, whether the loop closes.

And when the scene already exists? The depth estimator

We already know how to measure depth when we build the scene ourselves. And when the scene already exists — any photograph at all? That is where a depth estimator: a program that looks at a flat image and guesses, pixel by pixel, how far away each thing was.

It hands back the same 2.5D map from the previous station — and it is out of that map that the next six images come.

What the ruler needs to find in the photograph: well-placed contrast

Opening the image up shows what the estimator has to work with: the luminance — the brightness of each pixel, from 0 (black) to 1 (white), with the colour set aside — separates the paper from the ground with room to spare (0.48 of difference); and the edges come out enhanced: the gradient, the size of the jump in brightness from one pixel to its neighbour, reaches 0.32 at the silhouette.

the photograph has no texture at all: it is smooth paper. What it does have is a figure standing clear of the background, a sharp silhouette, and a shadow that anchors the piece to the plane. That is all the ruler needs.

If the geometry is stored in the map, the generative model may paint whatever we like on top — and the form stays the same. The six images that follow are that test.

Where all of this flows out: Guariba

Guariba is a work of fiction being born in the Pantanal — and all the mathematics in this seminar is its technical spine.

The same landscape, two waters: flood and drought. The same scene, two rulers — the whole seminar fits inside these two plates.

No artwork enters the work on impression alone: every candidate goes through blind judging (the images appear with no identification of origin — only what convinces on its own survives). The two plates above are survivors of those panels.

Concept art generated locally under the author's direction and curation — the machine proposes variation; invention and verdict are not delegated.

From the ruler to the work

What feeds the work: the hand

Nine pages — out of a notebook of fifty — drawn by hand. It is from this notebook, and from it alone, that the style of the work will be born: no model here learns from anyone else's work.

Drawing trains the hand; geometry trains the ruler; blind judging trains the eye. The three are the same study.

Normally the two crafts do not live in the same person — a scientist is not an artist, and an artist is not a scientist. The case here is a particular one, and not by gift: by the training this screen documents.

“A scientist is not an artist, and an artist is not a scientist — I am both.”

— Mateus Alkimim, author of Guariba

“Art and science have their meeting point in method.”

— Edward Bulwer-Lytton, Eugene Aram, 1832

It is the 1832 quotation that holds up the sentence above: if the meeting point is method, then being both is not a privilege — it is work. And work leaves a receipt: the pages beside it.

Closing

The same mathematics, from the record to the pixel

matrices → vectors → coordinates → equations → pixels

The question was how far. The answer has a name: one number per pixel — the distance to the first surface the ray meets.

The table of numbers from the first sheet is the same one that comes out at the end. What changed is that now we know who decides each number — and it is geometry, from beginning to end.

And the ruler does not carry everything: distance is not topology. Whoever knows what it measures — and what it leaves out — is whoever studied the geometry.
